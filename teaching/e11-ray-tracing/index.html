E11 / WHY LEARN THIS?

Why do the edge rays miss the focus?

A thin-lens sketch predicts one focus, but a real spherical lens has thickness and accepts rays over a finite aperture. Before changing its shape, you need a model that follows where those rays actually go and explains which approximation caused the mismatch.

What you gain: Build a surface-by-surface ray tracer, locate a practical image plane and identify the geometric image errors that motivate lens design.

Unpack the difficult steps

Why is solving the sphere equation not the whole intersection test?

1:01 · Replay this part

r(t) = r₀ + t d
|r(t) − C|² = R²

The quadratic equation describes the entire sphere. An optical prescription selects the cap containing its vertex, a clear aperture and an encounter order. Require a positive travel distance, the correct cap and a hit inside the aperture. Choose the nearest valid forward hit, rather than the larger root by habit.

How does a scalar angle law become a 3D direction update?

1:40 · Replay this part

n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂
|d| = 1

Split the incoming unit direction into components tangent and normal to the interface. Scale the tangential part by n₁/n₂. Unit length determines the transmitted normal component, with the sign chosen into the next medium. If the tangential part would have length greater than one, there is no real transmitted direction.

A sequential transmitted-ray tracer then reports total internal reflection. It does not continue an imaginary ray, and it does not resolve the evanescent electromagnetic field. A full wave model is needed for the latter question.

Why can two correct models give different focal positions?

2:32 · Replay this part

The paraxial lens matrix linearizes angles and heights. Exact surface intersections with vector Snell’s law retain finite-angle geometric effects. They should agree in the narrow on-axis limit, while wide-aperture rays can show spherical aberration. First compare the same reference planes: effective focal length and distance from the rear vertex are not interchangeable.

Both are geometric models. Neither produces diffraction merely by tracing more rays. Ray-density refinement tests numerical sampling; E03’s wave-based point response answers a different image-resolution question.

01 / BUILD AN INTUITION

What to notice first.

Start with the computed rays above. A ray records a position and a direction. In a uniform transparent medium it travels along a straight line; at a curved interface its direction changes according to the normal at the point it actually reaches.

r(t) = r₀ + t d,   |d| = 1,   t > 0

The algorithm has two separate questions: where does this ray meet the next surface, and which way does the transmitted ray leave? Solve the intersection first. A spherical surface has two algebraic intersections, but only the forward intersection on the specified surface cap belongs to this lens.

n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂

Both angles are measured from the local surface normal, not the optical axis. Refraction preserves the tangential component of the wavevector. The same rule works for a ray away from the meridional plane when expressed with vectors.

Here a collimated bundle enters a spherical glass singlet in air: R₁ = +50 mm, R₂ = −50 mm and centre thickness 4 mm. The refractive index defaults to n = 1.5. A positive radius places the sphere centre after its vertex along +z. The entrance stop is fixed at z = −5 mm; the image coordinate is always measured from the first vertex.

By the end, you can

  1. Find a forward surface intersection and apply Snell’s law using the local normal.
  2. Use a ray fan to test when a paraxial focal prediction stops being accurate.
  3. Explain why a geometric spot diagram does not establish diffraction-limited resolution.

03 / CONNECT THE LOGIC

A paraxial lens is the small-angle limit of this calculation.

For small heights and angles, sin θ and tan θ can be replaced by θ. Surface refraction and propagation then become linear operations. A thick-lens calculation gives an effective focal length and a back focal distance; they are measured from different reference planes and need not be equal.

1/f = (n − 1)[1/R₁ − 1/R₂ + (n − 1)d/(n R₁R₂)]

This lensmaker relation is paraxial, even though it includes centre thickness. The nonparaxial ray trace does not use it to bend each ray. Agreement as the entrance bundle becomes narrow is a useful independent check; disagreement at a wide aperture is often physical spherical aberration rather than a numerical bug.

A spot diagram records intersections, not a wave intensity.

The spot diagram places one dot where each sampled ray meets the image plane. Its centroid records image location; its root-mean-square radius records geometric spread about that centroid. The sampling and pupil weights must be stated before comparing numbers.

σ² = Σ wⱼ |xⱼ − x̄|² / Σ wⱼ,   x̄ = Σ wⱼ xⱼ / Σ wⱼ

A smaller aperture can reduce geometric aberration while increasing diffraction blur. This model has no wavelength-dependent interference, so a zero geometric spot is not a zero-width point-spread function. E03 supplies the wave-optical resolution question that the ray model leaves open.

A missing transmitted ray is a result to report.

A ray may miss a surface cap, hit outside the mechanical clear aperture, or undergo total internal reflection. The tracer reports these cases instead of silently including them in the spot. Fresnel power splitting, absorption, scattering and multiple reflected paths are outside this sequential transmitted-ray model.
